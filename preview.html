Demonstratives

This, that, these, those. One or many. Near or far.

Practice

You marked this topic to review.

The rule

Two things decide the word: how many, and how far.

NearFar
OneTHISTHAT
More than oneTHESETHOSE

This = one + near. That = one + far. These = several + near. Those = several + far.

See the difference

In a real sentence

This — one person or thing near you, or something you feel is close.

This is my phone. This book is interesting. I like this song. What is this? This is my friend.

That — one person or thing farther away.

That is my car. That building is very tall. What is that? I don't like that song. Look at that dog!

These — more than one, near you.

These are my keys. These books are mine. I like these shoes. What are these?

Those — more than one, far from you.

Those are my friends. Those cars are expensive. Look at those mountains. What are those?

Before a noun, or alone

The word can stand alone, or it can sit in front of a noun. The meaning stays the same.

This is my book. This book is mine.

That is my car. That car is fast.

These are my keys. These keys are mine.

Those are my shoes. Those shoes are expensive.

Not only distance

This can mean now: this week, this year, this morning.

That can mean a time already gone: that day, that year, that time.

In a conversation: What's this? What's that? Who's that? What are these? What are those?

Common mistakes

This books — wrong. This is for one thing, and books is more than one. Say these books.

These book — wrong. These is for more than one. Say this book.

That are my friends — wrong. That takes is, and it is for one. Friends is more than one, so say those are my friends.

Those is my car — wrong. Those takes are, and it is for more than one. A car is one, so say that is my car.

Student 1 · at home

Do you look at people around you and say to yourself, 'Wow, we're all so different!'? Well, recently I had the opposite idea. I think that in many ways we're all the same. We like, feel and do many of the same things.

What kind of things do we all do at home? We all dance in the kitchen to our favourite music and we look in the fridge for no reason. We put a key behind a book on a shelf or in a small box next to the computer. Then we forget where it is. We wake up early at weekends when we don't have work. And then we can't wake up early on Monday. We make a cup of tea or coffee, and then we forget to drink it until it gets completely cold. We stand in the shower for too long, thinking about life. We watch short videos online and tell ourselves, 'Just one more,' but then we are late for work. We keep clothes that we never wear. We look at our phone in bed, just before we go to sleep. And yes, sometimes we talk to ourselves, or to our pets.

Finally, we all have good days and bad days. We want people to like us. And we love being right.

Student 2 · outside

Do you look at people around you and say to yourself, 'Wow, we're all so different!'? Well, recently I had the opposite idea. I think that in many ways we're all the same. We like, feel and do many of the same things.

What about outside the home? Well, how do you feel about sitting in your car at a red light? Nobody likes waiting, so it's not surprising that we all hate traffic. In the cinema we all cry at the sad part of the film. We join gyms and pay a lot of money to be a member, and then we never go. We take a book to a café or the beach and never read it. And what about buying food? We go to the supermarket with a good plan, but we always buy crisps, chocolate and other snacks that are not on the list. We buy fresh vegetables because we want to eat healthily. Then we put them in the fridge and find them two weeks later, looking terrible.

Do you look everywhere for your phone and then find it's in your hand? Do you buy things and use them only once, and then never again? Do you pack extra clothes in your suitcase when you go on holiday? Do you only wear some of them, but not all of them? Of course you do. We all do. And which day of the week is your favourite? Don't tell me. I know the answer.

Monday. Adverbs of frequency. Level A2. This day's quiz uses only this day's cards.

#frequency#present-simple#how-often

14.09.2026.pdf

1. What you will learn

How often + Present Simple. Adverbs that say how regularly something happens, and phrases that say how many times. Two collocations: achieve a goal, beat others.

3. Grammar from this lesson

How often + Present Simple

How often asks about a habit, so the verb is Present Simple. Do/Does for ordinary verbs. Am/Is/Are when the sentence is with be.

How often do you check your phone? How often does your neighbour play loud music? Are you always so happy? Are you afraid of Monday mornings?

How often are you… is for a state with be. How often do you… is for an action.

4. Frequency expressions

always / usually / often / occasionally / rarely / hardly ever / never say how regular a habit feels. once a week / twice a week / 3 times a week / 3 times a year say a number of times. They answer different questions: “Do you usually…?” versus “How many times…?”

On one slide the words are “once in while”. The card uses the full form once in a while. most days, once a week, twice a week and 3 times a year sit on that slide and are not paired as answers to the adverb list.

Wednesday. Small talk. Level B1. How long have you…? is the harder piece. This day's quiz uses only this day's cards.

#small-talk#speaking#conversation#frequency#present-simple#present-perfect#polite-requests#starting-a-conversation#ending-a-conversation

16.09.2026.pdf

1. What you will learn

How to start, keep and end a short conversation with a stranger. How often + do/are. Polite requests with Do you mind if I…? Present Perfect in How long have you worked here?

2. Key phrases

Is anyone sitting here?

Translation: Здесь кто-нибудь сидит?

When to use: You want a free seat and you check first.

Useful variations: Excuse me, is anyone sitting here? · Is anyone using this? · Do you mind if I sit here?

Possible replies: No, go ahead. · Not at all. · Be my guest. The choice slides also show go on and you're. They do not mark one answer.

Example: Excuse me, is anyone sitting here?

Do you mind if I…?

Pattern: Do you mind if I + verb…?

Translation: Вы не против, если я…?

Do you mind if I sit here? · Do you mind if I charge my phone here? · Is it okay if I leave my bag here? · Is it OK if I use this?

Replies: No, not at all. = Нет, не против. Go ahead. = Да, пожалуйста. Be my guest. = Пожалуйста. Yes here can sound like “да, я против”.

How long have you worked here?

Pattern: How long + have/has + subject + past participle?

Translation: Как долго…?

How long have you lived here? · How long have you had your dog? · How long have you worked here? These alternatives are on the phrase slide.

3. Grammar from this lesson

How often + Present Simple

How often asks about a habit. An action takes do. A state with be takes are/is.

The gaps on the slide are still gaps: How often ___ ____ sit on benches? How often ___ ____ tired of walking?

A natural pair, using the same words: How often do you sit on benches? How often are you tired of walking?

The choice slide is How often are you \ do you, with the picture “… angry at work?”. It does not mark the button.

4. Frequency expressions

always, usually, often, occasionally, rarely, hardly ever, never describe the habit. once a month, twice a month, 5 times a year, most days and once in while give a number or a rough period. The scale writes once in while. The card on 14 Sep is once in a while.

Starting a conversation

Ending a conversation

7. Similar phrases

Go ahead. · Not at all. · Be my guest. · No problem. All of these can accept a request. No problem. is on the reply list and is not one of the 19 sort tiles.

How long do you worked here? How long have you worked here?

Do you mind if I sit here? — Yes. when you mean “я не против”. No, not at all. or Go ahead.

1 Who can sit there?

2 Why did she decide to create such benches?

Most of us spend part of our day surrounded by strangers, perhaps when travelling to work, sitting in a park or a café or visiting the supermarket. We are together with other people, but nobody talks. However, research shows that starting up a conversation with a stranger can make you feel happier and enjoy your day more.

When Allison Owen-Jones saw a man sitting alone on a bench in the park, she wanted to talk to him, but she felt uncomfortable because she wasn’t sure he would want to chat. Then she had an idea: ‘Happy to chat benches’. She made a sign to put on park benches which said, ‘Happy to chat bench. Sit here if you don’t mind someone stopping to say hello.’ The idea was a success. Now ‘Happy to chat benches’ can be found in several countries including Canada, the USA, Australia, Switzerland and Ukraine.

So, the next time you see someone sitting alone, give them a smile and say hello. You never know: they might be happy to talk.
